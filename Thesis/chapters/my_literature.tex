\section{Related Work/Simulation Based approaches}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

The optimal camera placement problem is known as the camera network design problem (CND) in photogrammetry. CND in photogrammetry has been a problem that has been investigated for a couple of decades. \citet{grafarend1974optimization} in the field of geodesy sought to optimise geodetic networks by generalising F.R Helmert's optimisation concepts and defined a classification scheme.  Whilst originally it pertained to the field of geodesy, photogrammetry professionals such as \citet{fraser1984network}, \citet{shortis1989network} and \cite{mason1994expert} leveraged the classfication scheme in considering their network design \citet{fraser1984network, shortis1989network, mason1994expert} showing the interrelated nature of geodesy network design optimisation and CND optimisation. This classified scheme for the optimisation of geodetic networks was divided into four orders of design problems: 

The zero order design (ZOD) problem, known as the datum problem is primarily to do with the choice of coordinate system for measurements. In CND optimisation this is the calibration step in which the world coordinate system is mapped out. 

The first order design (FOD) problem, the configuration problem (also known as the sensor placement problem), is the most complex in nature and persists to this day as finding the optimal configuration of the network. In CND has to do with the layout of the cameras in the network. 

The second order design (SOD) problem is the weighting problem or the densification problem. It asks, \textit{``What ought to be 
measured after designing an optimal configuration''}. It seeks to optimally distribute the observational work load. This translates to CND in how precise should the cameras be and what properties should they have such as: sensor resolution, the focal length and field of view to name a few. 

The third order design (TOD) problem also known as the bayesian approach or densification problem, asks \textit{what is the benefit of any additional measurement or observation in the network after reasoning about the quality of the present network?}. In CND, how does one improve the existing camera configuration by adding new cameras to the network.

\citet{grafarend1974optimization} stated that it is impossible to optimise geodetic networks without reviewing optimisation algorithms and distinguishing between direct or simulation methods. This statement in part aligns with \citet{fraser1984network}'s statement that the precision of the network is a function of its geometry which is determined in the design stage. 
\citet{fraser1984network} mentioned that computer simulation has been implemented successfully in the optimisation of photogrammetric network design (PND) and stated that there are a number of ways that computer simulation can be used for PND. Utilising the classification scheme by \citet{grafarend1974optimization}, \citet{fraser1984network} put forth a general method for using computer simulation in the process of photogrammetric network design optimisation.

\citet{mason1995expert} authored a paper titled \textit{Expert system-based design of close-range photogrammetric networks} designed an expert system -- as defined by \citet{denning1986towards} -- based network design system called CONSENS (CONfiguration of SENsors Stations). This work defined generic network design as achieving precise and reliable measurement of a desired object by configuring an optical triangulation network according to the classifcation scheme defined by \citet{grafarend1974optimization}. \citet{mason1995expert} states that the following three characteristics of a multi-camera network can have a large influence on it's triangulation accuracy.
The first is that observational redundancy -- that is the visibility of a keypoint in more than two views such that it can be triangulated -- leads to an improvement in measurement precision. The second is flexible camera positioning as to be adapted to suit the accuracy of the system and third is the convergence of multi-ray intersections, that is to say that the better both the distribution and convergence of the rays interesecting at a keypoint the better the triangulation and precision of the measurement will be. 
\citet{mason1995expert} highlighted the complexity of the FOD problem as there are a partically infinite number of locations in which the cameras can be placed. A trial and error approach to the FOD problem is insufficient as there is no certainity that all points of interest will be visible, some views may be redundant in that no new information is gained (e.g two cameras clustered closely together) and lastly that arbitrarily chosen cameras might not satisfy the accuracy requirements of the system.
\citet{tarbox1995planning} mapped their restricted form of the sensor placing problem, selecting a minimal number of cameras in a discretised search space to maximise coverage of a region,  onto the set-covering problem. The set-covering problem which was shown to be NP-complete by \citet{karp1972reducibility}, that is the algorithmic complexity is larger than polynomial complexity. \citet{mason1995expert} considers heuristic methods and that design-by-simulation is a term used by photogrammetric experts to define systems that have employed the use of heuristic methods.
Whilst the classification scheme by \cite{grafarend1974optimization} is helpful it does not give answers to the questions, \textit{how many cameras?} and \textit{where should the cameras placed?} \cite{mason1995expert}, \citet{mason1995expert} goes on to develop the CONSENS framework and architecture. They make use of two techniques of interest to this research the viewing sphere and the use of CAD. The placement of cameras is a viewing direction problem and using a volume sphere that is constrained they were able to use the unit sphere to ensure that cameras were a fixed distance from the object needing to be measured and the space was discretised so that the sphere was subdivdied into cells. The use of polar coordinates assisted the placement of the camera as two parameters determine the location, pan and tilt of the camera. The CAD system was useful for visualising the camera layout and added to the realism of the solution.
The limitations were that the object being measured was polyhedral in nature and not a human and secondly that it was not suitable to open world systems that is beyond the confinement of a known environment such as the interior of a machine.

The search for optimal CND solutions was studied further by experts such as \citet{olague2001applications}, \citet{gonzalez2009optimal}, \citet{zhao2013approximate}, \citet{rahimian2016optimal} and \citet{jorgensen2018simulation} to name a few. All the aforementioned experts conclude that camera placement impacts the performance of a multi-camera network. The placement of cameras in a network impact the network's success in accurately measuring objects and downstream computer vision tasks.
These experts consider various optimisation algorithms and evalute configurations according to some objective function and constraints in the attempts to minimise some error metric.

Both \cite{olague2001applications} and \cite{mason1995expert} consider viewing spheres that are constrained. This sets cameras a fixed distance from the object and causes cameras to face toward the center of the sphere. This allows camera placement to be defined by two numbers in polar coordinates given the unit sphere defining the pan tilt and location of the camera. 

An early paper by \cite{gonzalez2009optimal} sought to maximise the total coverage of a region by a multi-camera but later work by \cite{zhao2013approximate} stated that maximising coverage is no-longer sufficient in finding general optimal camera placement due to the varying performance objectives of varying camera networks comparing traffic detection and biomechanic pose detection requiring different setups.
\cite{zhao2013approximate} highlighted the complexity of the CND problem and that most researchers have resorted to discrete approaches. Researchers would discretise both the camera network as well as the camera environment. \citet{zhao2013approximate} compared and evaluted the accuracy and scalability of numerous optimisation algorithms namely greedy, greedy heuristics, Markov-Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC),Simualted Annealing, Linear and Semi-definite Programming relaxations. Zhao sought to solve two problems MIN and FIX. 
The MIN problem, defined as minimising the number of cameras such that a target coverage can be achieved subject to some constraints.
The FIX problem, definied as maximising the coverage of a subject to a fixed number of cameras and other constraints. 
Zhao's approach to both these problems is as mentioned above the discretisation of the camera configuration that is the location and rotation of cameras and their properties and secondly the discretisation of the target space, that is the objects position and orientations.
Zhao found that the greedy method found a good approximation for the MIN problem and focused the rest of the paper to solving for the FIX problem. To solve the FIX problem they mentioned that they could iteratively apply a solver for FIX for different number of cameras and search for a minimum that satisfies the target coverage rate. 
Zhao concluded that a hybrid of approaches to assist in the CND problem would be welcomed such as using the greedy algorithm to initialise camera placement over random camera placement initialisation. Zhao encouraged the further consideration of emergent optimisation techniques not covered in the paper.

\citet{rahimian2016optimal} introduced and compared two methods to position a set of cameras to maximise the visibility of keypoints with minimal 3D reconstruction error in the presence of dynamic occulsion (e.g from the motion of the subject). The first method is an occlusion and triangulability method that seeks to estimate the best views for a target group of keypoints. The seocond seeks to distribute the cameras such that the target keypoints are visible  from the widest range of viewing angles.
Rahimian pointed to sources of 3D motion capture failure namely: 3D reconstruction error, resolution degredation and dynamic occlusion. 
THe first, 3D reconstruction error is due to poor triangulability and uncertainty arising from the camera calibration process and the confidence of the keypoints location in the image. The when the keypoint is identified in two triangulable (non-parallel) camera views the rays from the sensor to the image point do not intersect precisely and normaly the midpoint between the two rays is taken. Based on the work done by \citet{olague2001applications} \citet{rahimian2016optimal} make use of a conservative 40-140 as the range of acceptable convergence angles that is to say triangulable.

THe second is due to the distance between the keypoint and the camera. The keypoint shrinks in size as the keypoint moves further away from the camera and this is true for any keypoint and camera. Therefore based on their personal setup they have a binary approach of a keypoint being visible or not dependent on the distance of the keypoint from the camera. 
The third being dynamic occulusion, Rahimians approach is to use probabilisitc occlusion modeling that takes both the resolution degredation and the triangulability. Rahimian models occluders as large panes of infinite occlusion dividing the space in two. The metric returns a number between 0 and 360 summing the occluder orientations where a keypoint is not visible in at least two triangulable views. This probabilistic occulision sum is then used in an occlusion based metric that is fed into the Simulated Annealing algorithm. In Simulated Annealing the algorithm for a given configuration only updates one camera at a time, this has no guarantee that the newly selected camera would have a keypoint that was previously untracked. They insert a penalty for keypoints that are unseen by any cameras so that the algorithm will be rewarded for camera placements that are update to see a previously unseen keypoint even if it is not triangulable yet.
Rahimian also recorded a two minute walk scene at 120Hz and took every 240th frame.

Rahimian concluded that the occlusion based method outperformed the distribution of camera views however noted that for live detection the distribution method was able to better in matching markers to skeletal keypoints than the occlusion based method.


\citet{jorgensen2018simulation} highlights the value that simulation based techniques hold over real-world testing. Every time a new layout is needing to be tested the simulation environment is updated and there is no need for rearrangement, recalibration, or retrials of motion capture where pose estimation is needing to be reevalutated. Jorgensen rightly noted that photo-realism rendering is computationally expensive and that often a trade off needs to be made between realism and rendering time. Using simulation is feasible to do many more experiments as the only cost is computation time when compared to the real-world where retrials are prone to unsystematic trial and error. The research contributed in that high success rates in the simulation of their experiments matched to high success rates in the real-world. They mention that for synthetic images to be used in the context of computer vision that two criteria need to be met that is namely: the iamges need to be sufficiently realistic and secondly that the computer vision algorithms need to exhibit similar behaviour for both the real and synthetic images.
