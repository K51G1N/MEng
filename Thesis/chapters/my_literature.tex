This chapter gives a broad sweeping overview of the literature that is relevant to solving the main objectives of this research
Simulation based network design optimisation. We look at existing synthetic human datasets as a means to assist us in setting up the synthetic environment for our simulation based approach. We look to human body modeling and human mesh recovery methods that have been implemented to best represent humans in a virtual environment. Turning back to simulation based approaches we look at optimisaation algorithms that are suited to our use case in simulation based camera optimisation for multi-view markerless pose estimation 
This chapter gives an overview and critique of the literature starting with simulation based approaches a brief chronological survey is done of the development of optimising camera network design starting with the classification scheme by \citet{grafarend1974optimization} and ending at \citet{mason1995expert}'s work in using it for photogrammetric network desing (PND). A critique on existing simulation based papers is given whilst highlighting the constraints used for the objective functions.


\section{Related Work/Simulation Based approaches}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

The optimal camera placement problem is known as the camera network design problem (CND) in photogrammetry. CND in photogrammetry has been a problem that has been investigated for a couple of decades. Arising at a similar time in the fields of computational geometry and geodesy with works by \citet{chvatal1975combinatorial} and \citet{grafarend1974optimization} respectively. Victor Klee introduced the Art Gallery problem (AGP) to Chvatal, seeking to answer what the minimum number of guards required to monitor an art  gallery that is an n-walled polygon such that every point is covered. \citet{chvatal1975combinatorial} provided a theorem that gives the upper bound as n/3 guards required. This covered important topics for computational geometry such as vertex covering and Delaunay triangulation and translated to the optimal camera placement problem \cite{o1987art}.
\cite{grafarend1974optimization}'s work in the field of geodesy sought to optimise geodetic networks by generalising F.R Helmert's optimisation concepts and defined a classification scheme.  Whilst originally it pertained to the field of geodesy, photogrammetry professionals such as \citet{fraser1984network}, \citet{shortis1989network} and \cite{mason1994expert} leveraged the classfication scheme in considering their camera network design, showing the interrelated nature of geodesy network design optimisation and CND optimisation. This classified scheme for the optimisation of geodetic networks was divided into four orders of design problems: 

The zero order design (ZOD) problem, known as the datum problem is primarily to do with the choice of coordinate system for measurements. In CND optimisation this is the calibration step in which the world coordinate system is mapped out. 

The first order design (FOD) problem, the configuration problem (also known as the sensor placement problem), is the most complex in nature and persists to this day as finding the optimal configuration of the network. In CND has to do with the layout of the cameras in the network. 

The second order design (SOD) problem is the weighting problem problem. It asks, \textit{``What ought to be 
measured after designing an optimal configuration''}. It seeks to optimally distribute the observational work load. This translates to CND in how precise should the cameras be and what properties should they have such as: sensor resolution, the focal length and field of view to name a few. 

The third order design (TOD) problem also known as the bayesian approach or densification problem, asks \textit{what is the benefit of any additional measurement or observation in the network after reasoning about the quality of the present network?}. In CND, how does one improve the existing camera configuration by adding new cameras to the network.

\citet{grafarend1974optimization} stated that it is impossible to optimise geodetic networks without reviewing optimisation algorithms and distinguishing between direct or simulation methods. This statement in part aligns with \citet{fraser1984network}'s statement that the precision of the network is a function of its geometry which is determined in the design stage. 
\citet{fraser1984network} mentioned that computer simulation has been implemented successfully in the optimisation of photogrammetric network design (PND) and stated that there are a number of ways that computer simulation can be used for PND. Utilising the classification scheme by \citet{grafarend1974optimization}, \citet{fraser1984network} put forth a general method for using computer simulation in the process of photogrammetric network design optimisation.

\citet{mason1995expert} authored a paper titled \textit{Expert system-based design of close-range photogrammetric networks} designed an expert system -- as defined by \citet{denning1986towards} -- based network design system called CONSENS (CONfiguration of SENsors Stations). This work defined generic network design as achieving precise and reliable measurement of a desired object by configuring an optical triangulation network according to the classification scheme defined by \citet{grafarend1974optimization}. \citet{mason1995expert} states that the following three characteristics of a multi-camera network can have a large influence on it's triangulation accuracy.
The first is that observational redundancy -- that is the visibility of a keypoint in more than two views such that it can be triangulated -- leads to an improvement in measurement precision. 
The second is flexible camera positioning as to be adapted to suit the accuracy of the system and third is the convergence of multi-ray intersections, that is to say that the better both the distribution and convergence of the rays interesecting at a keypoint the better the triangulation and precision of the measurement will be. 

\citet{mason1995expert} highlighted the complexity of the FOD problem as there is a practically infinite number of locations in which the cameras can be placed. A trial and error approach to the FOD problem is insufficient as there is no certainty that all points of interest will be visible, some views may be redundant in that no new information is gained (e.g two cameras clustered closely together) and lastly that arbitrarily chosen cameras might not satisfy the accuracy requirements of the system.

To further highlight the complexity of the FOD problem, \citet{tarbox1995planning} mapped their restricted form of the sensor placing problem, selecting a minimal number of cameras in a discretised search space to maximise the coverage of a region,  onto the set-covering problem. The set-covering problem was listed as NP-complete by \citet{karp1972reducibility}, that is the algorithmic complexity is larger than polynomial complexity. This stands in agreement with the work done by \citet{lee1986computational} who showed that finding the minimum number of cameras was proven to be NP-complete \cite{lee1986computational}.
It is clear that the optimal camera placement problem is a complex problem.

The search for optimal CND solutions was studied further by experts such as \citet{olague2001applications}, \citet{gonzalez2009optimal}, \citet{zhao2013approximate}, \citet{rahimian2016optimal} and \citet{jorgensen2018simulation} to name a few. All the aforementioned experts agree that camera placement impacts the performance of a multi-camera network. The placement of cameras in a network impact the network's success in accurately measuring objects and downstream computer vision tasks.
These experts consider various optimisation algorithms and evalute configurations according to objective functions and constraints in the attempts to minimise some error metric.

\citet{olague2001applications} highlighted that trial and error is an insufficient approach for finding optimal camera placement. Olague posed problem of CND in terms of optimisation design, breaking it into two steps the first being the analysis of error propogation in the system to find a criterion by which the second step of global optimisation would seek to minimise the criterion. Olague considered error in the 3D reconstruction process, 3D estimation process and error in image estimation of points to arrive at an error propogation based criterion. Olague poiinted out that due to occlusion in the system this introduced discontinuities and that it is a combinatorial optimisation process. The choice of algorithm was a multi-cellular genetic algorithms and inspired the use of genetic algorithms in later work conducted by \citet{aissaoui2018designing}. Olague chose the genetic algorithm due to their proven effeciency in searching non-linear and complex search spaces as well as genetic algorithms providing a generic framework for solving the combinatorial problem. This paper though only considers static objects and does not consider dynamic objects.

\citet{gonzalez2009optimal} attempts to solve the optimal camera placement problem for maximising coverage. Their objective was to find the optimal camera placement for a minimum number of cameras to cover a specific region, unlike \cite{olague2001applications} they considered constraints of both the camera and environment. This meant considering the camera's field of view and focal length and for the environment's size and shape. Gonzalez considers a mixture of both ominidirectional and directional cameras. They note that due to the poor resolution of omnidirectional cameras they are insufficient for object recognition. 
Whilst this paper wanted to maximise total coverage later work done by \citet{zhao2013approximate} states that maximising the coverage is no longer sufficient due to the varying applications each imposing their own performance objectives.

\citet{zhao2013approximate} mentioned that camera placement researchers have resorted to discretising both the physical environment and the camera network configuration search spaces due to the complexity of searching an otherwise continous and near infinite search space. The viewing sphere has been one method of discretising the camera network configuration search space, employed by \citet{mason1995expert}, \cite{olague2001applications} and \cite{rahimian2016optimal}. In particluar \cite{rahimian2016optimal} subsamples their footage that was shot at 120hz in only considering every 240th frame. The aforementioned researchers make use of a constrained viewing sphere setting the object to be measured at the centre of the sphere and placing cameras on the surface of a segmente sphere fixing the radius from the object to be measured and the location, pan and tilt of the cameras can be defined by two parameters alpha and beta in spherical coordinates since the radius is constant. 
\cite{zhao2013approximate}, \cite{rahimian2016optimal} and \citet{aissaoui2018designing} list a number of useful constraints and consideration in the context of camera placement planning. The first is the visibility constraint. In the context of multi-camera configurations it requires that a target keypoint is visible by at least $$k > 1 $$ cameras in the network. This ensures that reconstruction is possible enabling the recovery of lost depth information. 
The resolution degradation constraint termed partial coverage by \cite{zhao2013approximate} is the distance threshold beyond a camera's effective range. That is that a marker must be visible within a camera's resolution \cite{aissaoui2018designing}. Instead of a hard cutoff \citet{zhao2013approximate} implements a fuzzy model that can degrade from a keypoint being fully-visible to completely missing using a predefined threshold for the computer vision task. 
The third constraint is the group constraint which ensures that the set number of cameras are adhered to, not assigning more than one camera to the same physical location in the network.
The final constraint is the placement of stereo sensors or 3D point reconstruction by \citet{rahimian2016optimal}, this constraint minimses the number of cameras whilst guaranteeing that every keypoint is covered by a stereo pair. This ensures that a pair is triangulable, that is, a keypoint is visible by two cameras within a convergence angle range such that the angle suspended at the keypoint by the two cameras meet the baseline requirement. \citet{aissaoui2018designing} also enforce at cameras should be sufficiently non-parallel. \cite{rahimian2016optimal} put forth a conservative range of between 40-140 degrees based on the paper by \cite{olague2001applications} such that cameras are not parallel with one another sufficient for convergence. 
\cite{rahimian2016optimal} also discuss a probabilisitc occlusion model that feeds into an occlusion based error metric. They modeled an occluder as an infinite plane that would divide the viewing space in two, camera's on on the front side would consider the keypoint as visible whilst cameras on the back side would consider the keypoint occluded. 
Both \citet{zhao2013approximate} and \citet{rahimian2016optimal} considered the simulated annealing algorithm. Simulated annealing is an effective method for finding solutions close to the global optimal. Simulated annealing mimics the the cooling, annealing, of a heated metal. The annealing technique starts with some high temperature T, which represents the probability of accepting configurations with a higher error metric and as the temperature T cools the lower the probability of accepting a solution with a higher error metric until the objective function does not change overtime. The high temperature assists the algorithm of escaping local minima by selecting worse solutions initially with the hopes of cooling toward a global optimum.
The triangulation constraint is problematic for the SA algorithm as SA only changes a single camera at a time. This means that swapping out a camera does not necessarily mean that the new camera will benefit the system as keypoint might still only be visible by a single camera. \citet{rahimian2016optimal} combat this by adding a penalty for uncovered keypoints so that the algorithm is rewarded for adjusting a camera such that it covers a previously unseen keypoint, despite it still not being triangulable it is at least visible and thus rewarded. 
Whilst \cite{rahimian2016optimal} compared two methods an occlusion based method and a distribution based method, showed that for live capture scenarios that the distribution based method was better able to match markers to skeletal keypoints. In one particluar comparison where the widest distribution was known the distribution based method consistently found solutions close to the optimal solution but the occulsion based error metric tended to be planar in it's configuration. The authors speculate that it could be due to the nature of the occclusion based error metric, modeling occluders as infinte planes. It influences a cameras position on the same vertical plane either higher up or lower would receive the same occlusion-based error metric. 

\citet{jorgensen2018simulation} highlights the value that simulation based techniques hold over real-world testing. Every time a new layout is needing to be tested the simulation environment is updated and there is no need for rearrangement, recalibration, or retrials of motion capture where pose estimation is needing to be reevalutated. Jorgensen rightly noted that photo-realism rendering is computationally expensive and that often a trade off needs to be made between realism and rendering time. Using simulation is feasible to do many more experiments as the only cost is computation time when compared to the real-world where retrials are prone to unsystematic trial and error. The research contributed in that high success rates in the simulation of their experiments matched to high success rates in the real-world. They mention that for synthetic images to be used in the context of computer vision that two criteria need to be met that is namely: the iamges need to be sufficiently realistic and secondly that the computer vision algorithms need to exhibit similar behaviour for both the real and synthetic images. 



Both \cite{olague2001applications} and \cite{mason1995expert} consider viewing spheres that are constrained. This sets cameras a fixed distance from the object and causes cameras to face toward the center of the sphere. This allows camera placement to be defined by two numbers in polar coordinates given the unit sphere defining the pan tilt and location of the camera. 

An early paper by \cite{gonzalez2009optimal} sought to maximise the total coverage of a region by a multi-camera but later work by \cite{zhao2013approximate} stated that maximising coverage is no-longer sufficient in finding general optimal camera placement due to the varying performance objectives of varying camera networks comparing traffic detection and biomechanic pose detection requiring different setups.
\cite{zhao2013approximate} highlighted the complexity of the CND problem and that most researchers have resorted to discrete approaches. Researchers would discretise both the camera network as well as the camera environment. \citet{zhao2013approximate} compared and evaluted the accuracy and scalability of numerous optimisation algorithms namely greedy, greedy heuristics, Markov-Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC),Simualted Annealing, Linear and Semi-definite Programming relaxations. Zhao sought to solve two problems MIN and FIX. 
The MIN problem, defined as minimising the number of cameras such that a target coverage can be achieved subject to some constraints.
The FIX problem, definied as maximising the coverage of a subject to a fixed number of cameras and other constraints. 
Zhao's approach to both these problems is as mentioned above the discretisation of the camera configuration that is the location and rotation of cameras and their properties and secondly the discretisation of the target space, that is the objects position and orientations.
Zhao found that the greedy method found a good approximation for the MIN problem and focused the rest of the paper to solving for the FIX problem. To solve the FIX problem they mentioned that they could iteratively apply a solver for FIX for different number of cameras and search for a minimum that satisfies the target coverage rate. 
Zhao concluded that a hybrid of approaches to assist in the CND problem would be welcomed such as using the greedy algorithm to initialise camera placement over random camera placement initialisation. Zhao encouraged the further consideration of emergent optimisation techniques not covered in the paper.

\citet{rahimian2016optimal} introduced and compared two methods to position a set of cameras to maximise the visibility of keypoints with minimal 3D reconstruction error in the presence of dynamic occulsion (e.g from the motion of the subject). The first method is an occlusion and triangulability method that seeks to estimate the best views for a target group of keypoints. The seocond seeks to distribute the cameras such that the target keypoints are visible  from the widest range of viewing angles.
Rahimian pointed to sources of 3D motion capture failure namely: 3D reconstruction error, resolution degredation and dynamic occlusion. 
THe first, 3D reconstruction error is due to poor triangulability and uncertainty arising from the camera calibration process and the confidence of the keypoints location in the image. The when the keypoint is identified in two triangulable (non-parallel) camera views the rays from the sensor to the image point do not intersect precisely and normaly the midpoint between the two rays is taken. Based on the work done by \citet{olague2001applications} \citet{rahimian2016optimal} make use of a conservative 40-140 as the range of acceptable convergence angles that is to say triangulable.
% To combat the resolution degredation to try to get as close to the subject as possible without cutting off the subject. We define an aciton volume 
THe second is due to the distance between the keypoint and the camera. The keypoint shrinks in size as the keypoint moves further away from the camera and this is true for any keypoint and camera. Therefore based on their personal setup they have a binary approach of a keypoint being visible or not dependent on the distance of the keypoint from the camera. 
The third being dynamic occulusion, Rahimians approach is to use probabilisitc occlusion modeling that takes both the resolution degredation and the triangulability. Rahimian models occluders as large panes of infinite occlusion dividing the space in two. The metric returns a number between 0 and 360 summing the occluder orientations where a keypoint is not visible in at least two triangulable views. This probabilistic occulision sum is then used in an occlusion based metric that is fed into the Simulated Annealing algorithm. In Simulated Annealing the algorithm for a given configuration only updates one camera at a time, this has no guarantee that the newly selected camera would have a keypoint that was previously untracked. They insert a penalty for keypoints that are unseen by any cameras so that the algorithm will be rewarded for camera placements that are update to see a previously unseen keypoint even if it is not triangulable yet.
Rahimian also recorded a two minute walk scene at 120Hz and took every 240th frame.
